# Module 00 — Lesson 04: Focus, Sharpness, & Depth of Field

A photograph can have flawless exposure, perfect color, and zero noise—yet still be completely ruined if the subject is **out of focus**.

Unlike exposure (which can sometimes be nudged in post-processing), an unsharp, blurry subject cannot be recovered. In this guide, we demystify the optical mechanics of focus, explain what creates background blur (bokeh), and explore how OpenKamera assists your eyes with **Focus Peaking**.

---

## 1. The Focal Plane & Depth of Field

When a lens focuses on a subject, it establishes a theoretical two-dimensional plane in space called the **focal plane**. Anything resting precisely on this plane is rendered with maximum sharpness.

However, sharpness does not vanish instantly in front of or behind this plane. There is a zone of acceptable sharpness known as the **Depth of Field (DoF)**:

```
      ◄── IN FRONT ──►        [ FOCAL PLANE ]        ◄── BEHIND ──►
      (Foreground)             (The Subject)          (Background)
 
 ───┬──────────────────────┬────────────────────────┬──────────────────────┬───
    │ Blur / Out of Focus  │  ZONE OF ACCEPTABLE    │ Blur / Out of Focus  │
    │                      │   SHARPNESS (DoF)      │ (Bokeh Balls)        │
 ───┴──────────────────────┴────────────────────────┴──────────────────────┴───
                           ◄────────────────────────►
                             Depth of Field Window
```

### What Controls the Depth of Field?
Three physical factors govern how thin or thick this zone of sharpness is:

1. **Aperture ($f$-stop):**
   * Wide aperture ($f/1.4$, $f/1.8$) $\rightarrow$ Ultra-thin DoF (shallow focus, creamy blur).
   * Narrow aperture ($f/8$, $f/16$) $\rightarrow$ Deep DoF (front-to-back landscape sharpness).
2. **Subject Distance:**
   * The closer your camera is to the subject, the shallower the depth of field becomes. (This is why macro photography of flowers or insects has an extraordinarily razor-thin focus zone).
3. **Physical Sensor Size & True Focal Length:**
   * A larger sensor (like a $35\text{mm}$ full-frame DSLR) requires a physically longer lens (e.g. $50\text{mm}$ or $85\text{mm}$) to achieve the same field of view, producing intense optical blur.
   * A smartphone sensor is tiny, requiring a microscopic lens (typically $4\text{mm}$ to $6\text{mm}$ actual physical focal length). Because the physical focal length is so short, **smartphones have massive natural depth of field**. Everything from 1 meter away to the horizon stays naturally in focus!

## 2. Optical Bokeh vs Computational Bokeh

Because smartphones naturally have very deep depth of field, how do modern phones produce portraits with blurry backgrounds?

```
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐    ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
 │             Optical Bokeh            │    │         Computational Bokeh          │
 ├──────────────────────────────────────┤    ├──────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Natural physics of curved glass      │    │ Software simulation (AI / Dual Cam)  │
 │ Smooth, gradual falloff into blur    │    │ Hard boundary edge detection         │
 │ Naturally blurs foreground & back    │    │ Often glitches on fine hair / glasses│
 │ Zero battery or CPU rendering cost   │    │ Heavy neural network processing      │
 └──────────────────────────────────────┘    └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

On a smartphone:
* You can get **real optical bokeh** only by getting physically close to your subject (macro distance of $5\text{cm}$ to $15\text{cm}$) so that the background is relatively far away.
* Any background blur seen on people standing $3\text{ meters}$ away is generated by **computational segmentation masks** (Portrait Mode), not optical physics.

## 3. How Auto-Focus (AF) Actually Works

How does the camera know where to focus? Camera modules utilize two primary auto-focus technologies:

### A. Contrast-Detection AF (CDAF)
The camera moves the lens elements back and forth while inspecting the contrast of adjacent pixels in the preview stream.
* Sharp edges have high contrast (black pixels right next to white pixels).
* When out of focus, edges bleed into gray gradients (low contrast).
* **The drawback:** Contrast detection doesn't know *which direction* to move. It must "hunt" forward and backward past the sharp point and return. It is slow and struggles in low light or on flat walls.

### B. Phase-Detection AF (PDAF)
Modern smartphone sensors embed paired micro-lenses on specialized pixels across the sensor array.
* Incoming light rays from the left and right sides of the lens are compared.
* If the image is out of focus, the two light phases are separated by a measurable distance.
* **The advantage:** The sensor instantly knows **how far** and in **which direction** to move the lens motor in a single movement. It locks focus in milliseconds.

## 4. Manual Focus & Diopters in Camera2

Auto-focus is fast, but it can be tricked by rain on a window, branches in front of a bird, or low-light night skies. For complete creative control, photographers switch to **Manual Focus**.

In the Android Camera2 API, manual focus is measured in **Diopters** ($1 / \text{meters}$):

$$\text{Focus Distance (Diopters)} = \frac{1}{\text{Distance in Meters}}$$

* **$\text{Diopters} = 0.0$:** Focus is at **Infinity ($\infty$)** (stars, mountains, distant horizons).
* **$\text{Diopters} = 1.0$:** Focus is at **$1\text{ meter}$** away.
* **$\text{Diopters} = 10.0$:** Focus is at **$0.1\text{ meters}$ ($10\text{cm}$)** (macro close-up).

In OpenKamera, moving the manual focus slider directly sets:
```kotlin
captureRequestBuilder.set(CaptureRequest.CONTROL_AF_MODE, CameraMetadata.CONTROL_AF_MODE_OFF)
captureRequestBuilder.set(CaptureRequest.LENS_FOCUS_DISTANCE, diopterValue)
```

## 5. Focus Peaking: Highlighting Sharp Edges

Focusing manually on a small, reflective smartphone screen can be difficult. How can you be certain your subject's eyes are tack-sharp?

OpenKamera includes an essential professional tool: **Focus Peaking**.

```
                   FOCUS PEAKING IN ACTION
 
    [ Soft / Blurry Background ]         [ Tack-Sharp Subject ]
       No highlighted edges               Neon Green/Pink Outline
    ┌───────────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────────┐
    │          . . .            │       │         ▄████▄            │
    │        .       .          │       │       ▄█▓▓▓▓▓▓█▄ <────────┼── Neon highlight
    │                           │       │       █▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓█  (Sobel  │   drawn on high-
    │         (Trees)           │       │       █▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓█   filter)│   frequency edges
    │                           │       │       ▀█▓▓▓▓▓▓█▀          │
    └───────────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────────┘
```

### How Focus Peaking Works:
1. OpenKamera analyzes incoming frames through a high-pass spatial filter (such as a **Sobel edge detection kernel**).
2. It calculates the brightness gradient of neighboring pixels.
3. Wherever the rate of contrast change exceeds a designated threshold, those exact pixel boundaries are colored with a high-contrast neon hue (green, magenta, or yellow).
4. As you drag the manual focus slider, the neon shimmer sweeps across the scene, showing you the exact physical plane of focus in real time.

* **Learn more in code:** [`DrawPreviewContext.kt`](../../app/src/main/java/com/hightechif/openkamera/ui/renderers/DrawPreviewContext.kt) passes focus peaking parameters to the HUD rendering pipeline, and [`DrawPreview.kt`](../../app/src/main/java/com/hightechif/openkamera/ui/DrawPreview.kt) displays the edge highlights.
